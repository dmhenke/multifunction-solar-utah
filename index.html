Multifunction Solar on Utah's Public Waters and Working Lands

A research-first strategy for power generation paired with water conservation, dust suppression, and ecosystem enhancement.

Strategy Overview

Grand goal: Involve in solarizing public spaces where solar has more than one function — power generation paired with water conservation, dust suppression, or ecosystem enhancement.

The Correct Framing

Utah faces two simultaneous constraints: a ~2 GW-scale load growth problem (Operation Gigawatt), and a terminal-lake water crisis where agriculture accounts for the dominant share of consumptive depletion. Conventional solutions treat these as separate problems.

Multifunction solar — elevated over-water PV on engineered water bodies and agrivoltaics on irrigated cropland — is a class of infrastructure that produces both electrons and consumptively-saved water from the same capital investment.

The correct question: What is the marginal cost of a saved acre-foot and a delivered megawatt-hour, jointly, across the portfolio of candidate deployments — and which sites clear that bar?

Design Basis: Elevated Over-Water Arrays

Nothing rests on the water. Wherever an array is built over water, the modules sit on fixed racks several feet above the highest design water level, carried on piles fixed in the bed. The precedents are pile-mounted arrays over aquaculture ponds and reservoirs in China and the 1 GW HG14 offshore array in Shandong, which stands on fixed piles in 1–4 m of water.

Module Orientation Option: Vertical Bifacial

Every array can be built in either of two orientations. Option T (the baseline) tilts the modules 10–15° toward the south. Option V mounts two-sided (bifacial) modules vertically, facing east and west, in rows running north–south. Both faces generate, so output peaks in the morning and evening instead of at midday.

Why This Framing Works

  • Site-agnostic: You can never be dismissed as a promoter of one bad site
  • Quantitative: It survives contact with engineers
  • Coalition-friendly: It puts you on the same side as the Governor's energy office AND the Great Salt Lake Commissioner simultaneously

The Uncomfortable Truth You Must Build In First

Agrivoltaics on Utah farmland is a more powerful Great Salt Lake water intervention than solar over the Great Salt Lake itself.

  • Shading irrigated crops reduces crop evapotranspiration by 20–50% in arid climates
  • That is a reduction in consumptive use — water legally dedicable instream
  • Utah has a functioning legal channel: water-leasing and change-application framework

If you lead with "agrivoltaics is a water-generation technology for the Great Salt Lake," you have a genuinely novel, defensible, fundable thesis that nobody in Utah is currently owning.

The Depth Constraint — Decisive for This Site

Large pile-mounted over-water arrays stand in shallow water; HG14 uses 1–4 m (≈3–13 ft). Little Dell averages 82 ft deep. Pile foundations are therefore planned only where full-pool depth is ≲ 40 ft — the upper basin and the perimeter shelf. The rack underside sits at least 6 ft above the flood-control pool, not normal full pool.

Module Orientation Option

Vertical bifacial modules (Option V) would cut light under the array from about 60% to about 24% and the oxygen deficit from 0.41 to 0.18 mg/L, and they shed snow. Over dark water, though, they generate less than half the tilted output from the same footprint (≈18,700 vs ≈41,200 MWh/yr at full build-out), and matching tilted output would need ≈137 acres of qualifying shallow water. Recommendation: split Zone A into paired tilted and vertical blocks, so the demonstration measures water, light, oxygen, snow and energy for both. The Water Story — Read This First

Full build-out saves about 71 acre-feet per year (95% uncertainty interval 31–122) — about 0.08% (0.04–0.14%) of SLCDPU's annual water delivery. An elevated canopy saves less than a floating cover would, because wind still passes beneath it. See Quantitative Ecological Projections below.

Do not sell Little Dell as a water conservation project. The case for the project is:

  1. Distributed generation near load — existing access, existing land control, proximity to demand
  2. Water-quality intervention — reduced solar radiation at the surface suppresses algal production and may reduce disinfection-byproduct precursor loading
  3. Best-instrumented over-water PV research site in the Intermountain West — generate local evidence base

Critical Design Constraints

  1. Culinary source: Glass–glass modules only; 316 stainless or anodized-aluminium racks; precast concrete or stainless/duplex piles — no zinc galvanizing, coal-tar or biocidal coatings below water; power electronics onshore.
  2. Winter ice: Elevation keeps ice off the modules, but piles must resist sheet-ice thrust and ice-jacking (uplift as frozen-in ice rises with the stage).
  3. Water depth: Piles only where full-pool depth is ≲ 40 ft; rack underside ≥ 6 ft above the flood-control pool. Drawdown no longer affects the array — it does not move.
  4. Bonneville cutthroat brood stock: Statewide significance — no piles or canopy over spawning areas or dense macrophyte beds; drilled or vibratory pile installation outside spawning windows; telemetry monitoring.
  5. Removability is a requirement: Decommissioning bond, pile extraction or cut-off at the bed, and pre-agreed removal triggers written into approval.

Ecological Impact Report

Scope: Elevated, pile-mounted over-water PV — modules on racks several feet above the water, nothing resting on it: Zone A demonstration (12.45 ac, 5.0% coverage) and full build-out (62.45 ac, 25.1% coverage) on a culinary-supply reservoir in the Parleys Canyon Protected Watershed.

At demonstration scale (Zone A, 5% coverage) ecological effects are local, measurable and reversible by removal. Because the array is elevated, the water surface stays open to the air, so the oxygen penalty is roughly a third of what a floating array would cause. At full build-out (25% coverage) the effects that become material are bird perching with guano deposition into a culinary source, and the disturbance of installing up to a few thousand piles — sediment resuspension and underwater noise.

The single largest ecological hazard at this site is not the array itself. It is the introduction of invasive quagga or zebra mussels on construction barges, piles and equipment brought in from other waters. That risk is fully preventable and must be treated as a hard gate.

Verdict: Acceptable at demonstration scale with the mitigations below. Build-out beyond Zone A should be contingent on the Zone A guano, oxygen and pile-installation monitoring records — and on bathymetry showing enough water ≲ 40 ft deep to site piles.

≈ Wind

Baseline. Canyon setting with wind channelled along the Dell Creek / Parleys axis. Short fetch (reservoir ≈1.3 mi long), so wind-driven waves are small but wind remains the main driver of surface mixing and reaeration.

Mechanism. An elevated array shades the water but leaves a gap of several feet beneath the modules through which wind still moves. Sheltering is therefore weaker than under a floating array, where Lake Maiwald (Germany) field measurements recorded a 23% reduction in near-surface wind speed; treat that as an upper bound here.

  • Shading, more than wind sheltering, reduces evaporation beneath the array (beneficial, smaller than under a floating cover).
  • Wind-driven mixing and surface reaeration are largely preserved because the surface stays open — the main reason the oxygen penalty is much smaller than for a floating array (see Water).
  • Wave action is little changed; water flowing past the piles causes local scour around their bases (minor).

Mitigation. Keep racks open-sided, with no skirts, so air moves beneath the modules. Cap coverage at 25%. Orient array long axes parallel to the prevailing wind so open-water fetch is preserved between blocks. Maintain open-water gaps between Zones A, B and C rather than consolidating into one block.

Monitoring. Paired anemometers on the array and at control station C1; eddy-covariance mast for evaporation and momentum flux.

≋ Water

Baseline. Deep, cold reservoir (mean depth 82 ft, maximum ≈200 ft) expected to stratify thermally in summer — confirm against SLCDPU temperature-profile records. Culinary source with treatment downstream.

Mechanism. Under an elevated array shading dominates and wind sheltering is weak. Shading and wind sheltering act on stratification in opposite directions: reduced irradiance weakens it, reduced wind mixing stabilises it. The net effect is site-specific and coverage-dependent; modelling at Windermere suggests destabilisation becomes likely above roughly 45% coverage, well above the 25% cap here.

  • Evaporation: ≈14 (6–24) acre-ft/yr saved under Zone A, ≈71 (31–122) acre-ft/yr at full build-out, 95% uncertainty intervals (beneficial but small — ≈0.08% of SLCDPU's annual delivery). Less than a floating cover would save, because wind passes beneath the canopy.
  • Dissolved oxygen: floating arrays measure 1.1–1.7 mg/L lower beneath the panels. With the surface open to air, the projected deficit beneath an elevated array is about 0.4 (0.1–0.9) mg/L, from reduced photosynthesis in the shade rather than lost reaeration. Anoxic bottom water would still release manganese and iron at the intake, so the trigger stays (adverse, low).
  • Algal production: reduced light under the array suppresses phytoplankton, including bloom-forming cyanobacteria, and may lower disinfection-byproduct precursor loading (beneficial; magnitude unconfirmed).
  • Leachates: negligible if the culinary-source materials protocol is followed (glass–glass modules, precast concrete or stainless/duplex piles with no zinc or coal-tar coatings, 316 stainless racks, power electronics onshore). Only the piles touch the water.
  • Pile installation resuspends bed sediment and can mobilise nutrients and metals near the intake (adverse, short-term).

Mitigation. Site arrays away from the intake, in water shallow enough for piles (≲ 40 ft). Set a pre-agreed hypolimnetic DO trigger (e.g. sustained values below a level agreed with SLCDPU and Utah DWR) that requires aeration or array reduction. Drilled or vibratory pile installation inside silt curtains, or from the exposed bed at drawdown. Enforce the materials protocol in full.

Monitoring. Thermistor chains and multi-depth DO sondes at T1, T2 and C1; manganese and iron at the raw-water intake; TOC, DOC, UV254 and SUVA monthly; trace metals quarterly; 12-month pre-installation baseline.

✿ Flora

Baseline. Phytoplankton community; littoral macrophytes on the shallow shelf; riparian willow and cottonwood along Dell Creek; mixed Wasatch foothill slopes (Gambel oak, bigtooth maple, sagebrush — confirm by survey). Noxious weeds such as yellow starthistle are under active management in Salt Lake County.

Mechanism. Light reduction beneath the array — smaller than under a floating cover, because diffuse and side light reach the water; pile installation in the bed; physical disturbance at the shore transition vault, cable landing and onshore compound.

  • Phytoplankton biomass reduced beneath the array — fewer blooms, but also a smaller primary-production base for the food web (mixed).
  • Littoral macrophytes may be shaded, because the pile-depth limit pushes arrays toward the shallower margins (adverse, low–moderate).
  • Small, permanent loss of riparian or upland vegetation at the onshore compound and cable landing (adverse, low).
  • Construction traffic and fill can spread noxious weeds into a protected watershed (adverse, preventable).
  • Periphyton colonises the submerged pile surfaces, adding a little attached-algal habitat (mixed, small).

Mitigation. Map macrophyte beds with Utah DWR before siting; keep piles and canopy off dense beds and widen row spacing over the shelf. Place onshore works on the existing disturbed day-use pad. Weed-free certification for all equipment, fill and erosion-control materials. Revegetate disturbed ground with a native seed mix approved by SLCDPU watershed staff.

Monitoring. Pre- and post-construction vegetation transects at the landing and compound; chlorophyll-a and phycocyanin under array vs control; annual weed survey for three years.

✦ Fauna

Baseline. Bonneville cutthroat trout brood stock of statewide significance (catch-and-release), plus brook and brown trout; waterfowl and gulls using open water; big game (moose, elk, mule deer) typical of Wasatch canyon watersheds — confirm with Utah DWR.

Mechanism. Thermal and oxygen changes in the water column; piles as new submerged structure, with a canopy several feet above the water; pile-installation noise; construction disturbance.

  • Cutthroat thermal habitat: surface cooling under panels generally favours cold-water species, an effect the OSU/USGS multi-reservoir study found most pronounced above 50% coverage (beneficial, small at ≤25%).
  • Cutthroat oxygen habitat: a small DO deficit beneath the array; with the surface open to air it is far less likely to compress cold-water habitat than under a floating array (adverse, low).
  • Piles act as hard substrate and cover and attract fish, which can also concentrate predation (mixed).
  • Pile installation — impact driving especially — produces underwater noise that can injure or displace fish (adverse, short-term).
  • Gulls and cormorants will perch on rack edges several feet above the water, so guano — nutrients and faecal indicator bacteria — falls straight into a culinary source and soils modules (adverse, the most underrated issue at this site).
  • Waterfowl can still reach the water beneath the canopy, though some will avoid it (adverse, low).
  • Quagga/zebra mussel introduction on equipment from infested waters would be severe and effectively irreversible (adverse, potentially high).
  • Big-game disturbance limited to the construction window (low).

Mitigation. Mandatory Utah DWR aquatic-invasive-species decontamination and inspection for every barge, pile, rack component and tool entering the reservoir, with documented chain of custody. Bird-deterrent wire or spikes on array perimeters. Construction timed outside cutthroat spawning and waterfowl nesting windows; drilled or vibratory pile installation, with bubble curtains wherever impact driving is unavoidable. Co-design the telemetry study with Utah DWR.

Monitoring. Acoustic telemetry of tagged cutthroat; E. coli and total coliform at T1, T2, C1 and the intake; perching-bird counts and camera traps; veliger sampling for invasive mussels before and after construction; hydrophone monitoring during pile installation.

Quantitative Ecological Projections

How to read these intervals. Each value is a median with a 95% uncertainty interval: the 2.5th–97.5th percentiles of 100,000 Monte Carlo draws in which every uncertain input is sampled from a distribution set from the literature. No site measurements exist yet, so these are not sampling-based confidence intervals. Design basis: modules on racks several feet above the water, on piles — nothing rests on the water. Grades: A deterministic · B inputs from field measurements of the same design · C analogue transfer or structural prior — the interval reflects assumptions, not data. No elevated over-water array has been measured over a lake, so nothing here is graded B.

Key findings

  • Water saved is modest, and smaller than a floating cover would give. Full build-out saves 70.9 [31.4, 122] acre-ft/yr — about 10.9% of the reservoir's own evaporation and 0.080 [0.035, 0.139]% of SLCDPU's annual water delivery. Zone A saves 14.1 [6.27, 24.2] acre-ft/yr. Wind still passes beneath an elevated canopy, so suppression (≈40%) sits below the 44% measured under a full-scale floating array. No measurement exists for elevated arrays over a lake, so every water figure is grade C.
  • The oxygen penalty is small. With the water surface open to the air, the projected deficit beneath the array is 0.408 [0.086, 0.868] mg/L — about a third of the 1.1–1.7 mg/L measured under floating arrays. Across the whole reservoir it is 0.075 [0.015, 0.180] mg/L. The hypolimnetic DO trigger stays as a precaution.
  • Algae: direction is uncertain. Light under the panels falls by about 60.3% — less than under floats, because diffuse and side light reach the water. Measured chlorophyll-a responses at real over-water sites range from an 80% decrease to increases. Reservoir-wide primary production is projected to change -8.76 [-15.2, -4.17]% — fewer blooms, but less food at the base of the food web.
  • Piles are the new footprint. Zone A needs 309 [108, 648] piles; full build-out 1,548 [543, 3,249]. They occupy only 819 [194, 2,720] m² of bed at full build-out, but installing them resuspends sediment and makes underwater noise — managed with drilled or vibratory installation, silt curtains and timing.
  • Fish and insects: the model cannot tell you the sign. Cooling helps cold-water trout and oxygen loss hurts them; piles add cover and also concentrate predators. The intervals for trout habitat and insect emergence are the assumed ranges, centred near zero — read them as "unknown" until monitoring replaces them.
  • Vertical bifacial option. On the same footprint, vertical east/west-facing modules cut light under the array to 24.4 [11.2, 42.1]% and the oxygen deficit to 0.184 [0.036, 0.430] mg/L, and they shed snow. But over low-albedo water they generate only 18,744 [14,548, 23,681] MWh/yr against 41,220 [39,280, 43,144] tilted, and save half the water. Matching tilted output would take 137 [109, 176] acres — more water ≲ 40 ft deep than the reservoir is likely to have. Recommendation: test both orientations side by side within Zone A.

Birds and migration

  • Little Dell is not a major migration stopover. With racks several feet above the water, waterfowl can still use the water beneath the canopy (12.45 acres at Zone A, 62.45 at full build-out), though some will avoid it.
  • The material bird issue is perching: gulls and cormorants on rack edges drop guano — nutrients and faecal indicator bacteria — straight into a culinary source. Perimeter deterrent wire or spikes and coliform monitoring at the intake are the response. No defensible loading estimate exists without site bird counts.
  • Shielded, motion-activated lighting only at the onshore compound; no lighting on the racks.

What drives the uncertainty: for water saved, the evaporation-suppression coefficient beneath the elevated canopy (Spearman ρ = 0.93) dominates evaporation depth (0.30) and downwind sheltering (0.14). Measuring suppression at Zone A narrows every water number at once.

Recommended Strategy

Full document: Little Dell pre-feasibility site plan

Phase 4 site in the overall plan. Little Dell is deliberately NOT a first mover. The culinary-source framing that makes it attractive is the same framing that makes SLCDPU maximally risk-averse.

Optimal approach: Demonstrate on SLCDPU's own non-culinary surfaces first (treatment plant basins, backwash ponds, reclaimed-water storage). Then return to Little Dell in 3 years with SLCDPU's own operating data. This converts the hardest conversation into the easiest one.

Great Salt Lake — Multifunction Solar Pre-Feasibility Site Plan

Site: South-shore Gilbert Bay corridor | Jurisdiction: Sovereign lands, Utah FFSL | Stage (2026): ≈4,191.6 ft (6.4 ft below 4,198 ft healthy target)

Finding: Large-scale over-water photovoltaics on the open water of Great Salt Lake is not a viable proposition — whether floating or elevated on piles. The shallow lake suits piles (the 1 GW HG14 array in Shandong stands in 1–4 m of water), but four constraints defeat a large array: the lake's ~24 ft stage range (racks must clear the highest design stage, and every pile sits in the surface the lake must reclaim), materials (0.6 mm/yr steel corrosion in brine), ecology (unquantified shading effects on the food web), and public trust (a use with ecosystem risk is a poor candidate for trustee approval).

Recommended Strategy: Tiered Site Assessment

Rather than an open-water array, this plan proposes a two-part configuration:

  • Playa ground-mount array above the 4,200 ft contour as the primary generation asset
  • Small paired near-shore research module (F1), elevated on piles — rack underside at ≈4,204 ft, above the 4,200 ft limit — at research scale to answer brine-service and ecological questions; removed if the lake reaches 4,198 ft or after 5 years

Governing Design Principle

No infrastructure sited at Great Salt Lake may create an economic interest opposed to raising the lake.

A solar lessee holding a long-term interest in dry lakebed acquires a financial motive to oppose lake recovery. That is catastrophic. All development must be sited above the 4,200 ft contour (lake recovery reserve). Lease instrument must explicitly subordinate to elevation management and waive all inundation claims.

Module Orientation Option

The playa is where vertical bifacial modules make most sense. Salt crust reflects 30–50% of sunlight onto the rear faces, vertical modules shed dust, and their rows act as sand fences across the prevailing wind. On the same 1,600 acres they would carry ≈244 MW and generate ≈467,000 MWh/yr (tilted: 400 MW, ≈740,000 MWh/yr). Projected bird deaths fall to ≈439 per year, but per GWh the difference rests entirely on an assumed orientation effect. Recommendation: build P1 as paired 200-acre tilted and vertical blocks, so the Phase 1 avian and dust monitoring measures the orientation effect directly before P2 is designed.

The Water Story — Read This First

F1 suppresses about 5.6 acre-feet per year (95% uncertainty interval 2.5–9.7) of evaporation. Great Salt Lake evaporation is order 10⁶ acre-feet per year. F1 therefore addresses 0.0002% of lake evaporation. See Quantitative Ecological Projections below.

F1 is an experiment. It is not a water-conservation measure. Do not oversell the water benefit. F1's purpose is to generate brine-service durability and ecological data that any future over-water proposal in this system would require.

Critical Design Constraints

  1. Nothing may oppose lake recovery. No structure below 4,200 ft. Lake-elevation management takes precedence over lessee interest.
  2. Avian risk. Polarized-light "lake effect" attraction on the flyway is the governing ecological question for playa arrays.
  3. Saline materials. Corrosion exceeds 0.6 mm/yr in some steels. All hardware requires saline-service specification.
  4. Public trust primacy. Any sovereign-lands instrument is revocable to fulfil trust duties.
  5. Interconnection is the hard gate. Verify transmission capacity on the I-80 corridor before layout is fixed.

Ecological Impact Report

Scope: Playa ground-mount Zones P1 (400 ac) and P2 (1,200 ac) above the 4,200 ft contour, plus the 5-acre near-shore research module F1 — elevated on piles, rack underside at ≈4,204 ft, nothing resting on the water — in the south-shore Gilbert Bay corridor.

Covering no open water at scale removes the shading risk that defeats a lake-scale over-water array. That leaves avian interaction — collision, polarised-light attraction, and loss or degradation of open-playa nesting habitat — as the governing ecological question.

The dust co-benefit is real but probably modest at this corridor. University of Utah playa surveys place the principal dust hotspots in Farmington Bay, northern Bear River Bay, the northwest corner and Carrington Bay; the south-shore corridor does not appear among them. The hotspots are also largely below 4,198 ft or in the highest-value bird habitat, so relocating to chase dust benefit would trade against the lake-recovery principle and the flyway.

Verdict: Acceptable for Phase 1 (P1 + F1) only as a pre-registered study of avian effects. Phase 2 (P2) must remain formally contingent on that result.

≈ Wind

Baseline. Long open fetch across the lake and playa; prevailing south-westerly to westerly winds carry playa dust toward the Wasatch Front. Dust emission is controlled by wind shear on the surface and by the condition of the salt and biological crust.

Mechanism. Arrays raise aerodynamic roughness and physically shelter the ground beneath them, reducing near-surface wind speed and the shear stress that drives saltation. Construction can break the fragile surface crust that currently limits emission.

  • Reduced wind erosion and PM10 emission within the array footprint and a short distance downwind (beneficial; magnitude unquantified and likely modest at this non-hotspot corridor).
  • Crust disturbance during construction can temporarily increase dust emission — the opposite of the project's stated co-benefit (adverse, moderate, short-term).
  • Wind loading on ballasted foundations is an engineering rather than ecological issue, but foundation failure would scatter debris onto playa and reserve.

Mitigation. Ballasted, non-penetrating foundations to minimise crust disturbance. Restrict construction traffic to designated routes. Apply construction dust control at least as rigorously as operational dust claims. Phase construction to avoid high-wind seasons.

Monitoring. Upwind (D1) and downwind (D2) PM10 and PM2.5; dust chemistry including arsenic, which University of Utah sampling found above EPA residential and industrial screening levels; surface-roughness transects; wind profiles at M1.

≋ Water

Baseline. Lake at ≈4,191.6 ft, 6.4 ft below the 4,198 ft healthy target. Fringe springs and groundwater-dependent wetlands along the south shore. Hypersaline brine in Gilbert Bay.

Mechanism. P1 and P2 cover no water and sit above the Lake Recovery Reserve. F1 is the only structure over water, and it stands on piles above the surface. Effects therefore operate mainly through runoff, groundwater and the small F1 footprint.

  • Lake volume and recovery: not impeded — the reserve between current stage and 4,200 ft keeps all recoverable surface free of structures (neutral, high confidence).
  • F1 evaporation saving ≈5.6 (2.5–9.7) acre-ft/yr, roughly 0.0002% of lake evaporation (beneficial, negligible).
  • F1 shading reduces light to brine phytoplankton beneath 5 acres (adverse, negligible at lake scale).
  • Runoff concentrates at panel drip lines, causing rill erosion and local salt redistribution on the playa (adverse, low).
  • Panel washing with fresh water would add a consumptive demand in a water-scarce basin (adverse if permitted; avoidable).
  • Corrosion products from F1 piles and hardware enter brine locally (adverse, negligible; quantified by the coupon programme).

Mitigation. No freshwater panel washing — dry or robotic cleaning only. Drip-line erosion control with gravel strips. 300 ft buffers on springs and wetlands. Cable crossings of the reserve by directional bore only.

Monitoring. Spring discharge and conductivity at the wetland complex; erosion pins at drip lines; brine chemistry, PAR and chlorophyll-a at F1 versus control.

✿ Flora

Baseline. Open playa above the shoreline is largely unvegetated salt and silt crust, in places with biological soil crust. Margins support halophytes such as pickleweed, inland saltgrass and greasewood. Fringe wetlands are subject to invasion by Phragmites australis. In the lake, the food web rests on planktonic algae and benthic microbialites.

Mechanism. Physical disturbance during construction; altered light, moisture and temperature beneath panels; shading of brine under F1.

  • Little vegetation to lose on open playa itself (neutral).
  • Disturbance of halophyte margins where layout or access crosses them (adverse, low).
  • Breakage of salt and biological crust during construction (adverse, moderate, slow to recover).
  • Shade and drip-line moisture beneath panels may allow vegetation to establish. Native halophytes would aid dust control; Phragmites or other invasives would not (mixed).
  • F1 reduces light to phytoplankton and any microbialites beneath it (adverse, negligible at lake scale).

Mitigation. Avoid vegetated margins in final layout, and site F1 piles clear of microbialite structures. Active invasive-species management under arrays, with Phragmites control as an explicit operations obligation. Trial native halophyte establishment under a subset of panels as a dust-control experiment.

Monitoring. Annual vegetation cover and species composition under array versus control; crust integrity by repeat photogrammetry; benthic survey beneath F1.

✦ Fauna

Baseline. Roughly 12 million birds of more than 300 species use the lake annually. Reported to host up to 95% of North America's eared grebes during migration, along with large numbers of Wilson's phalaropes, American avocets, black-necked stilts and California gulls. Snowy plovers nest on open playa. Brine shrimp and brine flies form the base of the avian food web.

Mechanism. Collision with panels; attraction to polarised reflections that resemble water (the 'lake effect' hypothesis); loss of open-playa nesting ground; new perches that subsidise predators.

  • Avian mortality: a synthesis of 10 photovoltaic facilities in California and Nevada (Kosciuch et al., 2020) gave a high-end estimate of 2.49 fatalities per MW per year. Extrapolated to 402 MW that is roughly 720–1,000 birds per year at full build-out. That figure is transferred from desert sites and is probably an underestimate for water-associated birds on this flyway (adverse; magnitude moderate to high; confidence low).
  • Loss or degradation of open-playa nesting habitat for ground-nesting shorebirds such as snowy plover (adverse, moderate).
  • Panel frames and fences provide perches for ravens and raptors, increasing predation on nearby nests (adverse, low–moderate).
  • Aquatic insects, including brine flies, may oviposit on polarised panel surfaces instead of water (adverse, low; poorly studied).
  • Brine shrimp and brine fly density beneath F1 may fall locally (adverse, negligible at lake scale).
  • Reduced dust exposure for downwind wildlife and people (beneficial, low).

Mitigation. Co-design the P1 avian study with Utah DWR, USFWS and an academic ornithologist, with pre-registered fatality thresholds that gate P2. Use anti-reflective, non-polarising module surfaces and white edge-banding to break up continuous reflective area. Seasonal construction avoidance during nesting. Anti-perching hardware on frames and fences. Site P1 away from known plover nesting areas.

Monitoring. Standardised carcass searches with searcher-efficiency and scavenger-removal trials; avian radar at A1; nest surveys within 1 km; aquatic-insect trap arrays on and off the array; brine shrimp and brine fly density at F1.

Data gaps

  1. Corridor-specific bird-use surveys across all seasons, especially shorebird nesting locations.
  2. Dust emissivity of the specific corridor — the site's position relative to known hotspots needs field confirmation.
  3. Vegetation and biological soil crust mapping of the developable band above 4,200 ft.
  4. Spring and groundwater inventory along the south shore.
  5. Any published fatality data from PV facilities adjacent to large saline lakes or major waterbird concentrations.

Quantitative Ecological Projections

How to read these intervals. Each value is a median with a 95% uncertainty interval: the 2.5th–97.5th percentiles of 100,000 Monte Carlo draws in which every uncertain input is sampled from a distribution set from the literature. No site measurements exist yet, so these are not sampling-based confidence intervals. Design basis: modules on racks several feet above the water, on piles — nothing rests on the water. Grades: A deterministic · B inputs from field measurements of the same design · C analogue transfer or structural prior — the interval reflects assumptions, not data. No elevated over-water array has been measured over a lake, so nothing here is graded B.

Key findings

  • Solar over the water cannot meaningfully move the lake. F1 saves 5.64 [2.50, 9.74] acre-ft/yr — about 0.00011 inches of lake level per year. Offsetting just 1 ft of decline per decade would take 53,163 [30,790, 119,915] acres of elevated over-water PV, or 8.55 [4.86, 19.4]% of the current lake surface — roughly 12–48 GW of panels on piles. That is the clearest quantitative argument for prioritising upstream water, including agrivoltaics, over building on the lake.
  • The playa arrays may save more water than F1 does. Shading the playa reduces evaporation of shallow groundwater: 82.5 [21.4, 236] acre-ft/yr at full build-out, roughly an order of magnitude more than F1. Whether that water reaches the lake is unknown, and the estimate is prior-driven.
  • Birds are the governing impact, and the interval is wide. Projected fatalities at full build-out are 904 [177, 4,593] birds/yr. Almost all of that spread comes from one unmeasured number: how much higher fatality rates are beside a major waterbird concentration than at the desert sites the base rates come from (rank correlation 0.84). Phase 1 monitoring exists to measure exactly that.
  • Vertical bifacial is a strong candidate on the playa. Salt crust reflects 30–50% of sunlight, which favours two-sided vertical modules; they also shed dust and act as sand fences (57.5 [31.4, 83.6]% of footprint dust avoided vs 49.9 [21.5, 78.5]% tilted). Wider row spacing lowers output to 466,624 [357,821, 592,564] MWh/yr on the same 1,600 acres. Projected bird deaths fall to 439 [72.5, 2,595] per year, but per GWh the difference (0.941 [0.158, 5.50] vs 1.21 [0.238, 6.17]) rests entirely on an assumed orientation effect. Recommendation: build P1 as paired tilted and vertical blocks so the avian study measures it.

  • Mass grounding is the tail risk the fatality model does not capture. Eared grebes cannot take off from land. On 12 December 2011, a storm grounded thousands of migrating grebes on snow-covered, lit parking lots around St. George and Cedar City; roughly 1,500 died and 3,000–3,500 were rescued. A 1,600-acre dark, reflective array on the departure corridor is a plausible grounding surface under low cloud. One such event would equal about 1.7 years of the projected median background fatalities at full build-out, or about 6.7 years at P1. No defensible rate can be estimated; it should be treated as a design constraint, not a statistic.
  • Mitigations that target migration specifically: fragment the array with open corridors rather than one contiguous block; white edge-banding or gridding to break up water-like reflections; no array lighting and only shielded, motion-activated lighting at the substation; underground collector lines and marked (bird-flight-diverter) conductors on any new overhead gen-tie, since waterbirds are among the most power-line-collision-prone groups; and no construction during peak staging (July–August for phalaropes, September–November for grebes). F1's racks over the water are small, but get the same anti-perch and marking treatment.
  • Insect food supply: polarised reflections from panels draw aquatic insects to lay eggs on panels instead of water. In one field study, black-framed cells drew 6.9 times more mayfly landings than white-framed ones. Brine flies are a principal food for phalaropes and other shorebirds here, so white gridding serves both insects and birds. No interval is given: there is a single study and no brine-fly data.
