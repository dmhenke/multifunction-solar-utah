Pre-feasibility study

Multifunction Solar on Utah's Public Waters and Working Lands

A research-first strategy for power generation paired with water conservation, dust suppression, and ecosystem enhancement.

Strategy Overview

Grand goal: Involve in solarizing public spaces where solar has more than one function — power generation paired with water conservation, dust suppression, or ecosystem enhancement.

The Correct Framing

Utah faces two simultaneous constraints: a ~2 GW-scale load growth problem (Operation Gigawatt), and a terminal-lake water crisis where agriculture accounts for the dominant share of consumptive depletion. Conventional solutions treat these as separate problems.

Multifunction solar — floating PV on engineered water bodies and agrivoltaics on irrigated cropland — is a class of infrastructure that produces both electrons and consumptively-saved water from the same capital investment.

The correct question: What is the marginal cost of a saved acre-foot and a delivered megawatt-hour, jointly, across the portfolio of candidate deployments — and which sites clear that bar?

Why This Framing Works

  • Site-agnostic: You can never be dismissed as a promoter of one bad site
  • Quantitative: It survives contact with engineers
  • Coalition-friendly: It puts you on the same side as the Governor's energy office AND the Great Salt Lake Commissioner simultaneously

The Uncomfortable Truth You Must Build In First

Agrivoltaics on Utah farmland is a more powerful Great Salt Lake water intervention than floating solar on the Great Salt Lake itself.

  • Shading irrigated crops reduces crop evapotranspiration by 20–50% in arid climates
  • That is a reduction in consumptive use — water legally dedicable instream
  • Utah has a functioning legal channel: water-leasing and change-application framework

If you lead with "agrivoltaics is a water-generation technology for the Great Salt Lake," you have a genuinely novel, defensible, fundable thesis that nobody in Utah is currently owning.

The Water Story — Read This First

Full build-out saves about 111 acre-feet per year (95% uncertainty interval 56–180) — about 0.13% (0.06–0.21%) of SLCDPU's annual water delivery. See Quantitative Ecological Projections below.

Do not sell Little Dell as a water conservation project. The case for the project is:

  1. Distributed generation near load — existing access, existing land control, proximity to demand
  2. Water-quality intervention — reduced solar radiation at the surface suppresses algal production and may reduce disinfection-byproduct precursor loading
  3. Best-instrumented FPV research site in the Intermountain West — generate local evidence base

Critical Design Constraints

  1. Culinary source: No submerged polymer backsheets, no galvanized zinc, no antifouling coatings. Glass-glass modules only.
  2. Winter ice: Sheet-ice formation and movement is an unresolved mooring risk at this elevation.
  3. Seasonal drawdown: Mooring must accommodate full stage range without grounding the array.
  4. Bonneville cutthroat brood stock: Statewide significance — littoral and tributary zones excluded; telemetry monitoring required.
  5. Removability is a requirement: Decommissioning bond and pre-agreed removal triggers written into approval.

Ecological Impact Report

Scope: Floating PV: Zone A demonstration (12.45 ac, 5.0% coverage) and full build-out (62.45 ac, 25.1% coverage) on a culinary-supply reservoir in the Parleys Canyon Protected Watershed.

At demonstration scale (Zone A, 5% coverage) ecological effects are local, measurable and reversible by removal. At full build-out (25% coverage) two effects become material: reduced dissolved oxygen beneath the array, and bird perching with guano deposition into a culinary source.

The single largest ecological hazard at this site is not the array itself. It is the introduction of invasive quagga or zebra mussels on construction barges, floats and anchors brought in from other waters. That risk is fully preventable and must be treated as a hard gate.

Verdict: Acceptable at demonstration scale with the mitigations below. Build-out beyond Zone A should be contingent on the Zone A dissolved-oxygen and guano monitoring record.

≈ Wind

Baseline. Canyon setting with wind channelled along the Dell Creek / Parleys axis. Short fetch (reservoir ≈1.3 mi long), so wind-driven waves are small but wind remains the main driver of surface mixing and reaeration.

Mechanism. A floating array shelters the water surface. Field measurements at Lake Maiwald (Germany) recorded an average 23% reduction in near-surface wind speed at module height and a 73% reduction in irradiance reaching the water beneath the array.

  • Lower wind shear at the surface reduces evaporation beneath and immediately downwind of the array (beneficial).
  • The same sheltering reduces wind-driven mixing and surface reaeration, which is the mechanism behind lower dissolved oxygen under panels (adverse; see Water).
  • Reduced wave action on the adjacent shoreline marginally lowers shoreline erosion (minor, beneficial).

Mitigation. Cap coverage at 25%. Orient array long axes parallel to the prevailing wind so open-water fetch is preserved between blocks. Maintain open-water gaps between Zones A, B and C rather than consolidating into one block.

Monitoring. Paired anemometers on the array and at control station C1; eddy-covariance mast for evaporation and momentum flux.

≋ Water

Baseline. Deep, cold reservoir (mean depth 82 ft, maximum ≈200 ft) expected to stratify thermally in summer — confirm against SLCDPU temperature-profile records. Culinary source with treatment downstream.

Mechanism. Shading and wind sheltering act on stratification in opposite directions: reduced irradiance weakens it, reduced wind mixing stabilises it. The net effect is site-specific and coverage-dependent; modelling at Windermere suggests destabilisation becomes likely above roughly 45% coverage, well above the 25% cap here.

  • Evaporation: ≈22 (11–36) acre-ft/yr saved under Zone A, ≈111 (56–180) acre-ft/yr at full build-out, 95% uncertainty intervals (beneficial but small — ≈0.13% of SLCDPU's annual delivery).
  • Dissolved oxygen: field studies report DO 1.1–1.7 mg/L lower beneath FPV, with more frequent hypoxia. If bottom water goes anoxic, sediment can release manganese and iron, creating taste, odour and treatment burdens at the intake (adverse).
  • Algal production: reduced light under the array suppresses phytoplankton, including bloom-forming cyanobacteria, and may lower disinfection-byproduct precursor loading (beneficial; magnitude unconfirmed).
  • Leachates: negligible if the culinary-source materials protocol is followed (glass–glass modules, NSF/ANSI 61 floats, 316 stainless, no antifouling, power electronics onshore).
  • Construction turbidity at the shore landing and anchor placement (adverse, short-term).

Mitigation. Site arrays over deep water away from the intake. Set a pre-agreed hypolimnetic DO trigger (e.g. sustained values below a level agreed with SLCDPU and Utah DWR) that requires aeration or array reduction. Silt curtains during anchor placement. Enforce the materials protocol in full.

Monitoring. Thermistor chains and multi-depth DO sondes at T1, T2 and C1; manganese and iron at the raw-water intake; TOC, DOC, UV254 and SUVA monthly; trace metals quarterly; 12-month pre-installation baseline.

✿ Flora

Baseline. Phytoplankton community; littoral macrophytes on the shallow shelf; riparian willow and cottonwood along Dell Creek; mixed Wasatch foothill slopes (Gambel oak, bigtooth maple, sagebrush — confirm by survey). Noxious weeds such as yellow starthistle are under active management in Salt Lake County.

Mechanism. Light reduction beneath the array; physical disturbance limited to the shore transition vault, cable landing and onshore compound.

  • Phytoplankton biomass reduced beneath the array — fewer blooms, but also a smaller primary-production base for the food web (mixed).
  • Littoral macrophytes unaffected because the littoral shelf is excluded from the layout at minimum pool (neutral).
  • Small, permanent loss of riparian or upland vegetation at the onshore compound and cable landing (adverse, low).
  • Construction traffic and fill can spread noxious weeds into a protected watershed (adverse, preventable).
  • Periphyton colonising float undersides adds attached-algal habitat but also fouling load (mixed).

Mitigation. Place onshore works on the existing disturbed day-use pad. Weed-free certification for all equipment, fill and erosion-control materials. Revegetate disturbed ground with a native seed mix approved by SLCDPU watershed staff.

Monitoring. Pre- and post-construction vegetation transects at the landing and compound; chlorophyll-a and phycocyanin under array vs control; annual weed survey for three years.

✦ Fauna

Baseline. Bonneville cutthroat trout brood stock of statewide significance (catch-and-release), plus brook and brown trout; waterfowl and gulls using open water; big game (moose, elk, mule deer) typical of Wasatch canyon watersheds — confirm with Utah DWR.

Mechanism. Thermal and oxygen changes in the water column; new floating structure above and below the surface; construction disturbance.

  • Cutthroat thermal habitat: surface cooling under panels generally favours cold-water species, an effect the OSU/USGS multi-reservoir study found most pronounced above 50% coverage (beneficial, small at ≤25%).
  • Cutthroat oxygen habitat: lower DO beneath the array can compress usable cold-water habitat in late summer (adverse; the key fisheries uncertainty).
  • Float undersides act as cover and attract fish, which can also concentrate predation (mixed).
  • Gulls, cormorants and waterfowl will perch on array edges. Guano adds nutrients and faecal indicator bacteria to a culinary source and soils modules (adverse, the most underrated issue at this site).
  • Some displacement of waterfowl from covered open water (adverse, low).
  • Quagga/zebra mussel introduction on equipment from infested waters would be severe and effectively irreversible (adverse, potentially high).
  • Big-game disturbance limited to the construction window (low).

Mitigation. Mandatory Utah DWR aquatic-invasive-species decontamination and inspection for every barge, float, anchor and tool entering the reservoir, with documented chain of custody. Bird-deterrent wire or spikes on array perimeters. Construction timed outside cutthroat spawning and waterfowl nesting windows. Co-design the telemetry study with Utah DWR.

Monitoring. Acoustic telemetry of tagged cutthroat; E. coli and total coliform at T1, T2, C1 and the intake; perching-bird counts and camera traps; veliger sampling for invasive mussels before and after construction.

Data gaps

  1. SLCDPU temperature and DO profile records to establish stratification regime and existing hypolimnetic oxygen status.
  2. Utah DWR cutthroat spawning timing, habitat use and any existing telemetry data.
  3. Baseline waterbird use counts across seasons.
  4. Riparian and upland vegetation survey at the proposed landing and compound.
  5. Veliger baseline sampling to confirm the reservoir is currently mussel-free.

Quantitative Ecological Projections

How to read these intervals. Each value is a median with a 95% uncertainty interval: the 2.5th–97.5th percentiles of 100,000 Monte Carlo draws in which every uncertain input is sampled from a distribution set from the literature. No site measurements exist yet, so these are not sampling-based confidence intervals. Grades: A deterministic · B inputs from field measurements or validated models · C analogue transfer or structural prior — the interval reflects assumptions, not data.

Key findings

  • Water saved is about a quarter lower than first estimated. Full build-out saves 111 [56.4, 180] acre-ft/yr — about 17.1% of the reservoir's own evaporation and 0.126 [0.064, 0.205]% of SLCDPU's annual water delivery. Zone A saves 22.2 [11.2, 35.8] acre-ft/yr. The earlier ≈30 and ≈150 acre-ft/yr figures assumed 80% suppression under the panels; the only full-scale field measurement found reports 44%.
  • Algae: direction is genuinely uncertain. Light under the panels falls by roughly three-quarters, but measured chlorophyll-a responses at real FPV sites range from an 80% decrease to increases, depending on trophic state and mixing. Reservoir-wide primary production is projected to fall, which means fewer blooms but also less food at the base of the food web.
  • Oxygen is the effect to watch. The under-array deficit of about 1.2 mg/L is well supported by field data. Diluted across the reservoir it is small, but it concentrates where the array shelters the surface — exactly where a late-summer hypolimnetic DO trigger is needed.
  • Fish and insects: the model cannot tell you the sign. Cooling helps cold-water trout and oxygen loss hurts them; no field study has measured both at a comparable site. The intervals for trout habitat and insect emergence are the assumed ranges, centred near zero, and should be read as "unknown" until monitoring replaces them.

Great Salt Lake — Multifunction Solar Pre-Feasibility Site Plan

Site: South-shore Gilbert Bay corridor | Jurisdiction: Sovereign lands, Utah FFSL | Stage (2026): ≈4,191.6 ft (6.4 ft below 4,198 ft healthy target)

Finding: Large-scale floating photovoltaics on the open water of Great Salt Lake is not a viable proposition. Four independent constraints each individually defeat it: hydrodynamics (shallow, high-fetch basin), materials (0.6 mm/yr steel corrosion in brine), ecology (unquantified shading effects on food web), and public trust (a use with ecosystem risk is a poor candidate for trustee approval).

Recommended Strategy: Tiered Site Assessment

Rather than open-water FPV, this plan proposes a two-part configuration:

  • Playa ground-mount array above the 4,200 ft contour as the primary generation asset
  • Small paired near-shore FPV research module at research scale to answer brine-service and ecological questions

Governing Design Principle

No infrastructure sited at Great Salt Lake may create an economic interest opposed to raising the lake.

A solar lessee holding a long-term interest in dry lakebed acquires a financial motive to oppose lake recovery. That is catastrophic. All development must be sited above the 4,200 ft contour (lake recovery reserve). Lease instrument must explicitly subordinate to elevation management and waive all inundation claims.

The Water Story — Read This First

F1 suppresses about 9 acre-feet per year (95% uncertainty interval 4.5–14) of evaporation. Great Salt Lake evaporation is order 10⁶ acre-feet per year. F1 therefore addresses 0.0004% of lake evaporation. See Quantitative Ecological Projections below.

F1 is an experiment. It is not a water-conservation measure. Do not oversell the water benefit. F1's purpose is to generate brine-service durability and ecological data that any future FPV proposal in this system would require.

Critical Design Constraints

  1. Nothing may oppose lake recovery. No structure below 4,200 ft. Lake-elevation management takes precedence over lessee interest.
  2. Avian risk. Polarized-light "lake effect" attraction on the flyway is the governing ecological question for playa arrays.
  3. Saline materials. Corrosion exceeds 0.6 mm/yr in some steels. All hardware requires saline-service specification.
  4. Public trust primacy. Any sovereign-lands instrument is revocable to fulfil trust duties.
  5. Interconnection is the hard gate. Verify transmission capacity on the I-80 corridor before layout is fixed.

Recommended Sequencing

  1. NOW: Engage the Comprehensive Management Plan update (currently under revision)
  2. Months 0–12: Publish the tiered site-class assessment as peer-reviewed research
  3. Months 6–18: Build F1 first, at research scale, funded as research (FFSL Great Salt Lake Research Grants)
  4. Months 12–30: Verify interconnection capacity; run avian and glare studies for P1
  5. Year 2–3: Apply for P1 with studies complete and §4 covenants offered
  6. Year 4+: P2 only if P1 monitoring record supports it

Ecological Impact Report

Scope: Playa ground-mount Zones P1 (400 ac) and P2 (1,200 ac) above the 4,200 ft contour, plus the 5-acre near-shore FPV research module F1, in the south-shore Gilbert Bay corridor.

Covering no open water at scale removes the shading risk that defeats lake-scale floating PV. That leaves avian interaction — collision, polarised-light attraction, and loss or degradation of open-playa nesting habitat — as the governing ecological question.

The dust co-benefit is real but probably modest at this corridor. University of Utah playa surveys place the principal dust hotspots in Farmington Bay, northern Bear River Bay, the northwest corner and Carrington Bay; the south-shore corridor does not appear among them. The hotspots are also largely below 4,198 ft or in the highest-value bird habitat, so relocating to chase dust benefit would trade against the lake-recovery principle and the flyway.

Verdict: Acceptable for Phase 1 (P1 + F1) only as a pre-registered study of avian effects. Phase 2 (P2) must remain formally contingent on that result.

≈ Wind

Baseline. Long open fetch across the lake and playa; prevailing south-westerly to westerly winds carry playa dust toward the Wasatch Front. Dust emission is controlled by wind shear on the surface and by the condition of the salt and biological crust.

Mechanism. Arrays raise aerodynamic roughness and physically shelter the ground beneath them, reducing near-surface wind speed and the shear stress that drives saltation. Construction can break the fragile surface crust that currently limits emission.

  • Reduced wind erosion and PM10 emission within the array footprint and a short distance downwind (beneficial; magnitude unquantified and likely modest at this non-hotspot corridor).
  • Crust disturbance during construction can temporarily increase dust emission — the opposite of the project's stated co-benefit (adverse, moderate, short-term).
  • Wind loading on ballasted foundations is an engineering rather than ecological issue, but foundation failure would scatter debris onto playa and reserve.

Mitigation. Ballasted, non-penetrating foundations to minimise crust disturbance. Restrict construction traffic to designated routes. Apply construction dust control at least as rigorously as operational dust claims. Phase construction to avoid high-wind seasons.

Monitoring. Upwind (D1) and downwind (D2) PM10 and PM2.5; dust chemistry including arsenic, which University of Utah sampling found above EPA residential and industrial screening levels; surface-roughness transects; wind profiles at M1.

≋ Water

Baseline. Lake at ≈4,191.6 ft, 6.4 ft below the 4,198 ft healthy target. Fringe springs and groundwater-dependent wetlands along the south shore. Hypersaline brine in Gilbert Bay.

Mechanism. P1 and P2 cover no water and sit above the Lake Recovery Reserve. F1 is the only structure on water. Effects therefore operate mainly through runoff, groundwater and the small F1 footprint.

  • Lake volume and recovery: not impeded — the reserve between current stage and 4,200 ft keeps all recoverable surface free of structures (neutral, high confidence).
  • F1 evaporation saving ≈9 (4.5–14) acre-ft/yr, roughly 0.0004% of lake evaporation (beneficial, negligible).
  • F1 shading reduces light to brine phytoplankton beneath 5 acres (adverse, negligible at lake scale).
  • Runoff concentrates at panel drip lines, causing rill erosion and local salt redistribution on the playa (adverse, low).
  • Panel washing with fresh water would add a consumptive demand in a water-scarce basin (adverse if permitted; avoidable).
  • Corrosion products from F1 hardware enter brine locally (adverse, negligible; quantified by the coupon programme).

Mitigation. No freshwater panel washing — dry or robotic cleaning only. Drip-line erosion control with gravel strips. 300 ft buffers on springs and wetlands. Cable crossings of the reserve by directional bore only.

Monitoring. Spring discharge and conductivity at the wetland complex; erosion pins at drip lines; brine chemistry, PAR and chlorophyll-a at F1 versus control.

✿ Flora

Baseline. Open playa above the shoreline is largely unvegetated salt and silt crust, in places with biological soil crust. Margins support halophytes such as pickleweed, inland saltgrass and greasewood. Fringe wetlands are subject to invasion by Phragmites australis. In the lake, the food web rests on planktonic algae and benthic microbialites.

Mechanism. Physical disturbance during construction; altered light, moisture and temperature beneath panels; shading of brine under F1.

  • Little vegetation to lose on open playa itself (neutral).
  • Disturbance of halophyte margins where layout or access crosses them (adverse, low).
  • Breakage of salt and biological crust during construction (adverse, moderate, slow to recover).
  • Shade and drip-line moisture beneath panels may allow vegetation to establish. Native halophytes would aid dust control; Phragmites or other invasives would not (mixed).
  • F1 reduces light to phytoplankton and any microbialites beneath it (adverse, negligible at lake scale).

Mitigation. Avoid vegetated margins in final layout. Active invasive-species management under arrays, with Phragmites control as an explicit operations obligation. Trial native halophyte establishment under a subset of panels as a dust-control experiment.

Monitoring. Annual vegetation cover and species composition under array versus control; crust integrity by repeat photogrammetry; benthic survey beneath F1.

✦ Fauna

Baseline. Roughly 12 million birds of more than 300 species use the lake annually. Reported to host up to 95% of North America's eared grebes during migration, along with large numbers of Wilson's phalaropes, American avocets, black-necked stilts and California gulls. Snowy plovers nest on open playa. Brine shrimp and brine flies form the base of the avian food web.

Mechanism. Collision with panels; attraction to polarised reflections that resemble water (the 'lake effect' hypothesis); loss of open-playa nesting ground; new perches that subsidise predators.

  • Avian mortality: a synthesis of 10 photovoltaic facilities in California and Nevada (Kosciuch et al., 2020) gave a high-end estimate of 2.49 fatalities per MW per year. Extrapolated to 402 MW that is roughly 720–1,000 birds per year at full build-out. That figure is transferred from desert sites and is probably an underestimate for water-associated birds on this flyway (adverse; magnitude moderate to high; confidence low).
  • Loss or degradation of open-playa nesting habitat for ground-nesting shorebirds such as snowy plover (adverse, moderate).
  • Panel frames and fences provide perches for ravens and raptors, increasing predation on nearby nests (adverse, low–moderate).
  • Aquatic insects, including brine flies, may oviposit on polarised panel surfaces instead of water (adverse, low; poorly studied).
  • Brine shrimp and brine fly density beneath F1 may fall locally (adverse, negligible at lake scale).
  • Reduced dust exposure for downwind wildlife and people (beneficial, low).

Mitigation. Co-design the P1 avian study with Utah DWR, USFWS and an academic ornithologist, with pre-registered fatality thresholds that gate P2. Use anti-reflective, non-polarising module surfaces and white edge-banding to break up continuous reflective area. Seasonal construction avoidance during nesting. Anti-perching hardware on frames and fences. Site P1 away from known plover nesting areas.

Monitoring. Standardised carcass searches with searcher-efficiency and scavenger-removal trials; avian radar at A1; nest surveys within 1 km; aquatic-insect trap arrays on and off the array; brine shrimp and brine fly density at F1.

Data gaps

  1. Corridor-specific bird-use surveys across all seasons, especially shorebird nesting locations.
  2. Dust emissivity of the specific corridor — the site's position relative to known hotspots needs field confirmation.
  3. Vegetation and biological soil crust mapping of the developable band above 4,200 ft.
  4. Spring and groundwater inventory along the south shore.
  5. Any published fatality data from PV facilities adjacent to large saline lakes or major waterbird concentrations.

Quantitative Ecological Projections

How to read these intervals. Each value is a median with a 95% uncertainty interval: the 2.5th–97.5th percentiles of 100,000 Monte Carlo draws in which every uncertain input is sampled from a distribution set from the literature. No site measurements exist yet, so these are not sampling-based confidence intervals. Grades: A deterministic · B inputs from field measurements or validated models · C analogue transfer or structural prior — the interval reflects assumptions, not data.

Key findings

  • Floating solar cannot meaningfully move the lake. F1 saves 8.87 [4.51, 14.4] acre-ft/yr — about 0.00017 inches of lake level per year. Offsetting just 1 ft of decline per decade would take 33,834 [20,848, 66,577] acres of floating PV, or 5.43 [3.28, 10.8]% of the current lake surface — roughly 8–27 GW of panels at FPV power densities. That is the clearest quantitative argument for prioritising upstream water (including agrivoltaics) over covering the lake.
  • The playa arrays may save more water than F1 does. Shading the playa reduces evaporation of shallow groundwater: 82.0 [21.3, 236] acre-ft/yr at full build-out, roughly an order of magnitude more than F1. Whether that water reaches the lake is unknown, and the estimate is prior-driven.
  • Birds are the governing impact, and the interval is wide. Projected fatalities at full build-out are 911 [179, 4,595] birds/yr. Almost all of that spread comes from one unmeasured number: how much higher fatality rates are beside a major waterbird concentration than at the desert sites the base rates come from (rank correlation 0.84). Phase 1 monitoring exists to measure exactly that.

Birds and migration disruption

Species groupWhenWhy it matters here
Wilson's phalaropeStaging in early-to-late summer before migration to South AmericaThe lake is the species' principal staging site; one estimate holds up to 60% of the world population there at once.
Eared grebeFall staging September–November; departure late November–early January, after sunset on calm, clear nightsMonthly averages of about 968,000 (Sept), 320,000 (Oct) and 524,000 (Nov); long-run staging estimates of ~1.4 million (1997–2012 average) to 2.6–5.6 million (2013–2017 counts).
Shorebirds (avocet, stilt, snowy plover)Spring–summer breeding; snowy plover nests on open playaDirect overlap with playa array footprint — the primary habitat-loss pathway.
  • Mass grounding is the tail risk the fatality model does not capture. Eared grebes cannot take off from land. On 12 December 2011, a storm grounded thousands of migrating grebes on snow-covered, lit parking lots around St. George and Cedar City; roughly 1,500 died and 3,000–3,500 were rescued. A 1,600-acre dark, reflective array on the departure corridor is a plausible grounding surface under low cloud. One such event would equal about 1.6 years of the projected median background fatalities at full build-out, or about 6.6 years at P1. No defensible rate can be estimated; it should be treated as a design constraint, not a statistic.
  • Mitigations that target migration specifically: fragment the array with open corridors rather than one contiguous block; white edge-banding or gridding to break up water-like reflections; no array lighting and only shielded, motion-activated lighting at the substation; underground collector lines and marked (bird-flight-diverter) conductors on any new overhead gen-tie, since waterbirds are among the most power-line-collision-prone groups; and no construction during peak staging (July–August for phalaropes, September–November for grebes).
  • Insect food supply: polarised reflections from panels draw aquatic insects to lay eggs on panels instead of water. In one field study, black-framed cells drew 6.9 times more mayfly landings than white-framed ones. Brine flies are a principal food for phalaropes and other shorebirds here, so white gridding serves both insects and birds. No interval is given: there is a single study and no brine-fly data.

What drives the uncertainty: for bird fatalities, the unmeasured Great Salt Lake multiplier (ρ = 0.84) dominates the desert base rate (0.50). For the floating-PV area estimate, suppression again dominates (ρ = −0.84), ahead of downwind sheltering, brine salinity and evaporation depth.

Bottom line. At Little Dell the ecological effects are local and reversible at demonstration scale; the risks worth managing are lower oxygen beneath the panels, guano in a drinking-water source, and invasive mussels on construction equipment.
